### Abstract

In today’s technological landscape, writing processes have not just moved from the print paradigm to a multimodal born-digital creative writing space: they are increasingly supported or even driven by interactions with Generative AI (GenAI)’s Large Language Models (LLMs). These developments have implications on our interpretation of contemporary writing processes, and demand that genetic critics who study today’s texts engage with questions such as: What is the author’s input, and what is the chatbot’s? Should we regard the bot as an independent collaborator? Or should we just regard the LLM as another tool in the author’s toolbox? 

Our community has been ahead of the curve in these developments, studying computer-assisted writing processes since before the technology became widely available (Van Hulle 2023; reflecting on an experiment conducted in 2017). But with the arrival of ChatGPT, the floodgates have really opened. Chatbots no longer ‘simply’ generate text for the author to evaluate and incorporate or ignore: they can be prompted to compose intelligible longer-form texts that become increasingly indistinguishable from the real thing. Outside of the controlled environment of an experiment, new questions arise, and existing questions need to be tested to the more advanced technology. For example: How is the author’s collaboration with GenAI properly disclosed, if at all? Is prompt development enough to designate the resulting text as solely the authors’ creative work? And how do we encapsulate the iterative nature of the AI-assisted feedback loop into a comprehensive series of standalone versions? 

In my paper, I will illustrate this discussion with reflections on my experiences co-writing code with AI, co-editing an abstract, and the first results of an assignment I designed where students were allowed to co-write with AI if they distinguished its input from their own.
