### Abstract

In today’s technological landscape, our range of scholarly output is no longer confined to traditional academic publication formats like monographs and journal articles. Increasingly, scholars are creating digital objects for research purposes: scholarly code, algorithms, Jupyter Notebooks, curated datasets, etc., many of which are published on so-called “Git Hosting Platforms” like GitHub (Escamilla et al. 2022). At the same time, we are becoming more and more aware that when we publish these new, digital types of academic resources, it is not enough for the data itself to be of a high quality and follow the relevant standards and best practices; if we want our data to actually be found, understood, and reused, we will also need to embed it into a wider framework of contextual information. 

Taking a cue from the field of software engineering, whose products share a lot of similarities with these new types of scholarly output, much of this contextual information is usually called ‘documentation’: “formal writing […] that supports the efficient and effective use of software in its intended environment” (Ding et al. 2014, 546). Such documentation exists in many forms. To some extent, the software’s source code (which usually also incorporates explanatory source code comments) can be regarded as its own type of documentation – albeit arguably insufficient in isolation (Parnas 2011). Instead, software packages usually include more detailed descriptions of the software and its features in designated documents like README files, embedded documentation websites, or GitHub wikis (Puhlfürß et al. 2022). In addition, procedural documentation like commit messages and release notes describing distinct subsequent versions of the software helps us understand not just what its main features currently are, but also how these developed over time (Tian et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2024). As recent research by Isto Huvila and colleagues suggests, this type of ‘paradata’ (i.e., data describing the way in which digital data was made) may help to make our research data more useful for and reusable by others (Huvila 2022; Huvila et al. 2024). 

In an increasingly digital academic world, humanities and social science scholars are looking towards developments in software engineering and computer science, and adopting some of their tools, workflows, and practices. Still, there are currently no guidelines or best practices available to recommend researchers how to best develop this type of documentation. In this paper, I will draw lessons from research into software documentation, to explore how they may translate into a blueprint for a set of recommendations for humanities and social science scholars aiming to develop and publish their digital scholarly output on a Git Hosting Platform. Guiding questions in this research include: Which types of documentation should I prioritise? What should I keep in mind when I write this documentation? What are the challenges and opportunities of outsourcing some of these writing tasks to Generative AI? and How can I make sure that both my digital scholarly output and its documentation are archived in a long-term sustainable way?
